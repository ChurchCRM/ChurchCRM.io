ChurchCRM is free, open-source, self-hosted software that helps churches manage their congregation — people, families, groups, giving and the everyday work of the office — so staff can spend less time on paperwork and more time on ministry. There is no subscription, and the data stays with each church.

We've been open source since 2015, and we still do this as a small, mostly volunteer effort. I maintain the project part-time, around a full-time job. What has changed is the help around us.

## From records to real church workflows

In the early years, ChurchCRM was mostly a place to add, edit and delete records. That's still the foundation, but the product has grown into workflow-based tools that follow how a church office actually works — the front desk and the back office, not just a database.

Since November last year, as AI became part of our daily work, the pace of that work has changed in a way the numbers make plain. In the past 12 months we published 50 releases, up from 11 the year before, and made 4,457 commits on the main branch, up from 1,539. We now release about every week, so a feature a church asks for can reach other churches within days, and we keep closing security holes as we go.

## People can join in, even without a deep tech stack

Our contributors come from around the world. In the past month, 16 people made their first contribution.

One of AI's practical benefits is that any church member with an AI subscription can draft a change and submit it without knowing the tech stack deeply. That has cut the friction from idea to feature, week after week. We built ChurchCRM so AI can help a new contributor get oriented, make a change, and ship it in a regular release.

## Not only code

The reach isn't just engineering. AI helped us create a new website at [churchcrm.io](https://churchcrm.io), keep the documentation and manuals at [docs.churchcrm.io](https://docs.churchcrm.io) current, and draft marketing reports. This article was written with AI assistance too.

We also treat AI as an experimentation platform, not a single coding assistant. We started with GitHub, then added Claude, ChatGPT, Grok, Muse, GrokBot, Gemini and Copilot. We deliberately run code, writing and reviews through several different AI styles so no single model's idea or bias makes it through unchanged. That variety is how we keep quality up.

Community support works the same way. In Discord, NanoClaw offers 24/7 help in the requester's language, even while I sleep. When someone files a ticket, a bot opens it and points them to a place to follow progress. Those real questions have made debugging easier and the install process friendlier, because the documentation gets updated from what people actually ask.

Localization has always been a big part of serving churches well. ChurchCRM ships with 49 locales, and many of those translations now come from AI.

## A small budget, a wide door

I don't have a large staff. AI is what lets a one-person, part-time operation produce at this scale.

The heart of the story is not a sales pitch. AI in church software is here to help the mission of the church, whatever someone's background, location or role. It also helps contributors of faith build skills they can put on a resume, which is part of why new people keep joining.

It's a small budget with a big reach, a service for churches worldwide, and the data stays with each church.

If ChurchCRM would help your congregation, you can try it at [churchcrm.io](https://churchcrm.io) or see the project on [GitHub](https://github.com/ChurchCRM/CRM).
